# WeChat Official Accounts

## Platform reality

WeChat Official Account articles travel through private social graphs. Readers usually encounter them on a phone, often through a direct share or a Moments repost rather than open-web discovery.

Key constraints:

- Most readers scan with one hand and can leave at any moment.
- A typical article gets only a few minutes of attention unless the value is unusually strong.
- Trust comes mainly from the person who shared the article.
- Sharing is an identity signal: the article says something about the person forwarding it.

Articles are most likely to spread when they:

- make the sharer look informed, perceptive, useful, or entertaining;
- express a thought the sharer felt but could not formulate;
- provide a practical tool, method, or resource;
- trigger recognition, surprise, anger, hope, or another strong emotion.

## Writing mindset

Write as if speaking to a smart friend with very little patience. Do not lecture, warm up slowly, or sound like institutional documentation.

Assume the reader:

- is filling a spare moment;
- is always ready to swipe away;
- needs a fresh reason to continue every few seconds;
- wants insight and utility, not instruction from above.

The article should:

- earn attention in the opening lines;
- give every paragraph a reason to exist;
- end with a reason to save, discuss, or share it.

## Anti-patterns

Readers leave immediately when an article:

- opens with broad historical background or a generic industry trend;
- uses academic framing such as "This article will explore...";
- presents long mobile paragraphs or an unbroken wall of text;
- delays the central claim until after several screens.

Readers may finish but not share when an article:

- has no memorable claim or independently useful excerpt;
- takes a position so safe that it adds nothing;
- fades into a weak conclusion;
- gives no natural next action or discussion point.

Readers feel patronized when an article:

- explains common knowledge at length;
- uses a superior or moralizing tone;
- repeatedly tells the reader what they "should" do without earning authority.

## Structure

### Opening

The first screen should create tension, specificity, or immediate utility.

Weak:

> As digital transformation continues to accelerate, organizations face many new challenges.

Stronger:

> Yesterday, I deleted an app I had used for five years.

> Most teams misunderstand the real cost of this decision.

> The short answer: do not use this approach yet.

### Body rhythm

Optimize for a narrow mobile screen:

- Prefer short paragraphs.
- Vary sentence length to avoid a mechanical rhythm.
- Use whitespace as pacing, not decoration.
- Bold only claims worth pausing on.
- Use lists to compress structure, not to disguise weak prose.
- Add quotations or callouts only when they create a meaningful visual break.

Keep the argument moving. Every few paragraphs, introduce one of the following:

- a consequence;
- a contradiction;
- a concrete example;
- a new question;
- a useful takeaway;
- a change in emotional intensity.

### Ending

Close with one strong move:

- a concise line that captures the argument;
- an open question that deserves discussion;
- a concrete action the reader can take;
- a useful summary worth saving.

Do not stack several calls to action or add a generic motivational paragraph after the real ending.

## Headline design

A WeChat headline must compete inside a dense mobile feed. Prefer concrete tension over abstract importance.

Useful patterns:

- **Specific contrast**: show two outcomes, behaviors, or groups with a meaningful gap.
- **Counter-position**: challenge advice the target audience commonly accepts.
- **Consequence**: connect a small decision to a surprisingly large result.
- **Recognition**: name a situation the audience immediately sees in itself.
- **Curiosity with substance**: withhold one element without hiding the actual topic.

Avoid:

- academic titles;
- generic reflections on a broad topic;
- excessive length;
- abstractions without a person, event, decision, or number;
- clickbait that the article does not repay.

## Expert checks

### Hook density

Every three or four sentences should contain a reason to continue. A hook may be a reversal, unresolved consequence, promised explanation, concrete benefit, or unusually clear observation. Do not turn every sentence into manufactured suspense.

### Emotional curve

Strong articles rarely stay emotionally flat:

- the opening creates tension or recognition;
- the body alternates evidence, insight, and smaller peaks;
- the ending releases, resolves, or sharpens the original tension.

### Screenshot value

Some passages should remain useful when separated from the article. This does not mean manufacturing slogans. It means expressing a complete, defensible insight compactly enough that a reader would want to keep or share it.

## Final quality test

Before publishing, ask:

- Does the first screen work without background explanation?
- Does every section advance the argument or reader value?
- Can the core idea be summarized in one sentence without making the article redundant?
- Are the memorable lines earned by evidence rather than forced into slogan form?
- Would someone with no relationship to the author finish, save, or share it? Why?
